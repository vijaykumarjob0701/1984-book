## Summary

The street turns sharply and ends in steps down to a sunken alley where a few stall-keepers sell tired vegetables. Winston suddenly knows where he is: the alley leads to the main street, and the next turning, not five minutes away, is the junk-shop where he bought the blank book that is now his diary. Nearby he also bought the penholder and ink. At the top of the steps he pauses. Opposite is a dingy little pub whose windows look frosted but are only coated with dust. A very old man, bent but active, with white moustaches bristling like a prawn's, pushes the swing door and goes in. Winston realises the man must be at least eighty and was already middle-aged when the Revolution happened. He and a few like him are the last links to the vanished capitalist world. In the Party almost nobody still has ideas formed before the Revolution: the older generation was wiped out in the fifties and sixties purges, and the few survivors were terrified into total intellectual surrender. If anyone alive could give a truthful account of the early century, it could only be a prole. The children's-book passage he copied returns, and a mad impulse takes him: he will go in, scrape acquaintance with the old man, and ask what life was like when he was a boy — better or worse than now? Hurrying so fear cannot stop him, he goes down and across. It is madness. There is no definite rule against talking to proles or using their pubs, but the act is far too unusual to pass unnoticed.
