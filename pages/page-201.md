## Summary

When he wakes it is with the sensation of having slept a long time, but a glance at the old-fashioned clock tells him it is only twenty-thirty. He lies dozing for a while; then the usual deep-lunged singing strikes up from the yard below — the same drivelling popular song about a hopeless fancy, still in the prole woman's cockney voice. The song seems to have kept its popularity. You still hear it all over the place. It has outlived the Hate Song. Julia wakes at the sound, stretches herself luxuriously, and gets out of bed. She is hungry. Let's make some more coffee. Damn! The stove has gone out and the water is cold. She picks the stove up and shakes it. There is no oil in it. They can get some from old Charrington, Winston expects. The funny thing is she made sure it was full. She is going to put her clothes on; it seems to have got colder. Winston also gets up and dresses. The indefatigable voice sings on — they say that time heals all things, they say you can always forget, but the smiles and the tears across the years still twist the heart-strings. As he fastens the belt of his overalls he strolls across to the window. The sun must have gone down behind the houses; it is not shining into the yard any longer. The flagstones are wet as though they had just been washed, and he has the feeling that the sky has been washed too, so fresh and pale is the blue between the chimney-pots. Tirelessly the woman marches to and fro, corking and uncorking herself, singing and falling silent, and pegging out more diapers, and more and yet more. He wonders whether she takes in washing for a living or is merely the slave of twenty or thirty grandchildren. Julia has come across to his side; together...
