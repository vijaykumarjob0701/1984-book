## Summary

For a couple of minutes he feels he will die if he does not reach a lavatory. There would be no public ones in a quarter like this. The spasm passes, leaving a dull ache. The street is a blind alley. He halts, wonders what to do, then retraces his steps. As he turns he realises the girl passed only three minutes ago: if he ran he could catch her, keep on her track till a quiet place, and smash her skull with a cobblestone — or use the heavy glass. He drops the idea at once. Even the thought of physical effort is unbearable. He cannot run or strike. Besides, she is young and lusty and would defend herself. He also thinks of hurrying to the Community Centre until closing, to build a partial alibi. That too is impossible. A deadly lassitude has taken hold. All he wants is to get home, sit down, and be quiet. It is after twenty-two hours when he reaches the flat. Lights go off at the main at twenty-three thirty. In the kitchen he swallows nearly a teacup of Victory Gin, then sits at the alcove table and takes out the diary but does not open it. A brassy female voice on the telescreen is squalling a patriotic song. He stares at the marbled cover, trying and failing to shut the voice out. They come for you at night, always at night. The proper thing is to kill yourself first. Some people do; many disappearances are actually suicides. But it needs desperate courage in a world where firearms and any quick poison are completely unprocurable. He thinks with astonishment of how biologically useless pain and fear are — of the body's treachery, freezing into inertia at the exact moment a special effort is needed. He might have silenced the dark-haired girl if only he had acted quickly...
