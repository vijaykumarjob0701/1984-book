## Summary

...gent. For perhaps twenty seconds he sits without stirring. Then he pulls the speakwrite toward him and raps out a message in the hybrid jargon of the Ministries — approving some items, calling another suggestion doubleplus ridiculous and verging on crimethink, and cancelling construction until fuller estimates arrive. He rises deliberately and comes toward them across the soundless carpet. A little of the official atmosphere seems to have fallen away with the Newspeak words, but his expression is grimmer than usual, as though he is not pleased at being disturbed. The terror Winston already feels is suddenly shot through by ordinary embarrassment. It seems quite possible he has simply made a stupid mistake. What evidence has he, in reality, that O'Brien is any kind of political conspirator? Nothing but a flash of the eyes and a single equivocal remark: beyond that, only his own secret imaginings, founded on a dream. He cannot even fall back on the pretence of borrowing the dictionary, because then Julia's presence is impossible to explain. As O'Brien passes the telescreen a thought seems to strike him. He stops, turns aside, and presses a switch on the wall. There is a sharp snap. The voice has stopped. Julia utters a tiny sound, a sort of squeak of surprise. Even in the midst of his panic, Winston is too much taken aback to hold his tongue. "You can turn it off!" he says. Yes, says O'Brien: they can turn it off. They have that privilege. He is opposite them now. His solid form towers over the pair, and the expression on his face is still indecipherable. He is waiting, somewhat sternly, for Winston to speak — but about what? Even now it is quite conceivable he is simply a busy man wondering irritably why he has been interrupted. Nobody speaks. After the stopping of the telescreen the room seems deadly silent.
