In the whole of May they succeed in making love only once more, in another hide-out Julia knows: the belfry of a ruined church in almost-deserted country where an atomic bomb fell thirty years earlier. Once you are there it is good; getting there is very dangerous. For the rest they can meet only in the streets, a different place every evening, never more than half an hour. In the street they can usually talk, after a fashion. Drifting down crowded pavements, not quite abreast and never looking at one another, they carry on a curious intermittent conversation that flicks on and off like a lighthouse — nipped into silence by a Party uniform or a nearby telescreen, picked up minutes later mid-sentence, cut short at the agreed spot, continued next day almost without introduction. Julia is used to this; she calls it "talking by instalments." She is also surprisingly adept at speaking without moving her lips. Just once in almost a month of nightly meetings they manage a kiss. They are passing in silence down a side-street (she will never speak off the main streets) when a deafening roar heaves the earth, the air darkens, and Winston finds himself on his side, bruised and terrified. A rocket must have dropped close. Suddenly Julia's face is a few centimetres from his, deathly white, as white as chalk — even her lips. She is dead! He clasps her and finds he is kissing a live warm face. Powdery stuff gets in the way: both faces are thickly coated with plaster. There are evenings when they reach the rendezvous and must walk past without a sign, because a patrol has just turned the corner or a helicopter is hovering.
