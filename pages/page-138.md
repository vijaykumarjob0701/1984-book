## Summary

...an old couple thought to be of foreign extraction have their house set on fire and die of suffocation. In the room over Charrington's shop, when they can get there, Julia and Winston lie side by side on a stripped bed under the open window, naked for coolness. The rat has not come back, but the heat has multiplied the bugs. It does not seem to matter: dirty or clean, the room is paradise. They sprinkle black-market pepper, tear off their clothes, make love, fall asleep, and wake to find the bugs massing again. They meet four, five, six — seven times in June. Winston has stopped drinking gin at all hours; he no longer needs it. He is fatter; the varicose ulcer has subsided to a brown stain; the morning coughs have stopped. Life is no longer intolerable. He has no impulse to pull faces at the telescreen or shout curses. Now that they have an almost-home, even meeting only for a couple of hours does not feel like a hardship. What matters is that the room exists. Knowing it is there, inviolate, is almost the same as being in it. It is a pocket of the past where extinct animals could walk. Charrington, Winston thinks, is another extinct animal. Winston usually stops to talk with him on the way up. The old man almost never goes out and has almost no customers. He lives a ghostlike life between the dark shop and a tinier back kitchen with an ancient gramophone and an enormous horn.
