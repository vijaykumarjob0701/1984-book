## Summary

...enough: but precisely because the danger was extreme he lost the power to act. In a crisis, he thinks, you never fight an outside enemy so much as your own body. Even now, despite the gin, the dull belly-ache makes consecutive thought impossible. It is the same in all seemingly heroic or tragic situations. On the battlefield, in the torture chamber, on a sinking ship, the issues you are fighting for are forgotten, because the body swells up until it fills the universe. Even when you are not paralysed by fright or screaming with pain, life is a moment-to-moment struggle against hunger, cold, sleeplessness, a sour stomach, or an aching tooth. He opens the diary. It is important to write something. The woman on the telescreen has started a new song; her voice sticks into his brain like jagged splinters of glass. He tries to think of O'Brien, for whom or to whom the diary is written, but instead imagines what will happen after the Thought Police take him. Being killed at once would not matter; that is what you expect. But before death — nobody speaks of it, everybody knows — there is the routine of confession: grovelling, screaming for mercy, the crack of broken bones, smashed teeth, bloody clots of hair. Why endure it, if the end is always the same? Why can you not cut those days or weeks out of your life? Nobody ever escapes detection; nobody ever fails to confess. Once you have succumbed to thoughtcrime, you will be dead by a given date. Why then must that horror, which alters nothing, lie embedded in future time? He tries again, a little more successfully, to summon O'Brien. They shall meet in the place where there is no darkness. He thinks he knows what it means: the imagined future you will never see, but can mystically share by foreknowledge. But the nagging telescreen voice will not let him follow the thought...
