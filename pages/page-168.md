## Summary

...paper cylinders has covered the desk like a snowdrift, half-burying the speakwrite and overflowing onto the floor, so that the first job is always to stack them into a neat enough pile to give him room to work. Worst of all, the work is by no means purely mechanical. Often it is enough merely to substitute one name for another, but any detailed report of events demands care and imagination. Even the geographical knowledge needed to transfer the war from one part of the world to another is considerable. By the third day his eyes ache unbearably and his spectacles need wiping every few minutes. It is like struggling with some crushing physical task, something one had the right to refuse and which one was nevertheless neurotically anxious to accomplish. In so far as he has time to remember it, he is not troubled that every word he murmurs into the speakwrite, every stroke of his ink-pencil, is a deliberate lie. He is as anxious as anyone else in the Department that the forgery should be perfect. On the morning of the sixth day the dribble of cylinders slows down. For as much as half an hour nothing comes out of the tube; then one more cylinder, then nothing. Everywhere at about the same time the work is easing off. A deep and as it were secret sigh goes through the Department. A mighty deed, which could never be mentioned, has been achieved. It is now impossible for any human being to prove by documentary evidence that the war with Eurasia had ever happened. At twelve hundred it is unexpectedly announced that all workers in the Ministry are free till tomorrow morning. Winston, still carrying the brief-case containing the book — it has remained between his feet while he worked and under his body while he slept — goes home, shaves, and almost falls asleep in his bath, although the water is barely more than tepid. With a sort of voluptuous creaking in his joints he climbs the stair above Mr Charrington's shop. He is tired, but not sleepy any longer. He opens the window, lights the dirty little oilstove, and puts on a pan of water for coffee. Julia will arrive presently: meanwhile there is the book. He sits down in the sluttish armchair and undoes the straps of the brief-case.
