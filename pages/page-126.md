## Summary

Chapter 4 opens in the shabby little room above Mr Charrington's shop. The enormous bed is made up with ragged blankets and a coverless bolster. The twelve-hour clock ticks on the mantelpiece. In the corner, on the gateleg table, the glass paperweight from his last visit gleams in the half-dark. In the fender are a battered tin oilstove, a saucepan, and two cups, provided by Charrington. Winston lights the burner and sets water to boil. He has brought Victory Coffee and saccharine. The clock says seventeen-twenty; it is really nineteen-twenty. She is coming at nineteen-thirty. Folly, folly, his heart keeps saying: conscious, gratuitous, suicidal folly. Of all Party crimes this is the least possible to conceal. The idea first floated in as a vision of the paperweight mirrored on the table. As he foresaw, Charrington made no difficulty about letting the room; he is obviously glad of the few dollars. He did not seem shocked or become offensively knowing when it was clear Winston wanted it for a love-affair. Instead he looked into the middle distance and spoke in generalities, so delicately he seemed partly invisible. Privacy, he said, was very valuable. Everyone wanted a place to be alone occasionally, and it was only common courtesy for anyone who knew of it to keep that knowledge to himself. Almost fading out of existence, he added that there were two entries, one through the back yard into an alley. Under the window somebody is singing. Winston peeps out, safe behind the muslin curtain. The June sun is still high. In the sun-filled court below a monstrous woman, solid as a Norman pillar, with brawny red forearms...
