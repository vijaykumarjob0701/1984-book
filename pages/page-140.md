## Summary

Sometimes they talk of active rebellion, but they have no idea how to take a first step. Even if the fabulous Brotherhood is real, the problem is finding a way in. Winston tells her of the strange intimacy he feels, or thinks he feels, with O'Brien, and of the impulse to walk in, announce that he is the Party's enemy, and ask for help. Surprisingly, she does not find that impossibly rash. She judges people by faces, so a single flash of the eyes seems enough reason to trust O'Brien. She also takes it for granted that nearly everyone secretly hates the Party and would break the rules if it felt safe. What she will not believe is that widespread, organised opposition exists or could exist. The Goldstein stories, she says, are Party rubbish you have to pretend to believe. Times beyond number she has shouted for the execution of people whose names she has never heard and whose supposed crimes she does not believe. At public trials she has stood with Youth League detachments chanting "Death to the traitors!" During the Two Minutes Hate she always outdoes everyone insulting Goldstein, yet she has only the dimmest idea who he is or what he is supposed to teach. She grew up after the Revolution and cannot remember the ideological battles of the fifties and sixties. An independent political movement is outside her imagination. The Party is invincible: it will always exist and always be the same. You can only rebel by secret disobedience or, at most, isolated violence — killing someone or blowing something up. In some ways she is far more acute than Winston, and far less susceptible to Party propaganda. Once, when he mentions the war against Eurasia, she startles him...
