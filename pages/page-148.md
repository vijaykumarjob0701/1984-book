## Summary

...distance — above all, the fact that there was never enough to eat. He remembers long afternoons with other boys scrounging round dustbins and rubbish heaps, picking out cabbage-leaf ribs, potato peelings, sometimes even scraps of stale breadcrust from which they scraped the cinders; and waiting for trucks known to carry cattle feed, which sometimes spilt a few fragments of oil-cake when they jolted over bad patches. When his father disappeared, his mother showed no surprise or violent grief, but a sudden change came over her. She seemed completely spiritless. Even to Winston it was evident she was waiting for something she knew must happen. She did everything needed — cooked, washed, mended, made the bed, swept, dusted — always very slowly and with a curious lack of superfluous motion, like an artist's lay-figure moving of its own accord. Her large shapely body seemed to relapse naturally into stillness. For hours she would sit almost immobile on the bed, nursing his young sister, a tiny, ailing, very silent child of two or three, with a face made simian by thinness. Very occasionally she would take Winston in her arms and press him against her for a long time without saying anything. He was aware, despite youth and selfishness, that this was somehow connected with the never-mentioned thing that was about to happen. He remembers the room: dark, close-smelling, seeming half filled by a bed with a white counterpane. There was a gas ring in the fender, a shelf for food, and on the landing a brown earthenware sink common to several rooms. He remembers his mother's statuesque body bending over the gas ring to stir a saucepan. Above all he remembers continuous hunger, and the fierce sordid battles at mealtimes. He would ask naggingly why there was not more food, shout and storm (he even remembers the tones of a voice beginning to break, sometimes booming in a peculiar way), or attempt a snivelling note of pathos to get more than his share.
