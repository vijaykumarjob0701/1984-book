## Summary

To Winston's left, a little behind, someone talks in a harsh, nonstop gabble like a duck, cutting through the room's noise. Winston asks how the Dictionary is going. Syme brightens at once: he is on adjectives and finds it fascinating. He leans in so he need not shout. The Eleventh Edition will be the final shape of the language, the only one anyone will speak; people like Winston will have to learn it again. The team's main job is not inventing words but destroying them daily, cutting language "to the bone." By 2050 nothing in that edition should be obsolete. He eats hungrily, then continues with a pedant's passion; his face becomes almost dreamy. Destroying words is beautiful, he says. Verbs and adjectives waste most space, but many nouns can go too — not only synonyms but antonyms. Why have a word that is merely the opposite of another? A word contains its opposite. If you have "good," you do not need "bad": "ungood" is a cleaner opposite. For stronger "good," skip "excellent" and "splendid"; use "plusgood" or "doubleplusgood." Those forms already exist, but in final Newspeak...
