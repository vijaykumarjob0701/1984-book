...dropped his brief-case. The one the man gives him will contain a copy of Goldstein's book. He will return it within fourteen days. They are silent for a moment. There are a couple of minutes before Winston need go, O'Brien says. They shall meet again — if they do meet again —— Winston looks up. "In the place where there is no darkness?" he says hesitantly. O'Brien nods without appearance of surprise. He repeats the phrase as though he had recognized the allusion. And in the meantime, is there anything Winston wishes to say before he leaves? Any message? Any question? Winston thinks. There does not seem to be any further question he wants to ask; still less does he feel any impulse to utter high-sounding generalities. Instead of anything directly connected with O'Brien or the Brotherhood, there comes into his mind a sort of composite picture of the dark bedroom where his mother spent her last days, and the little room over Charrington's shop, and the glass paperweight, and the steel engraving in its rosewood frame. Almost at random he says: did O'Brien ever happen to hear an old rhyme that begins "Oranges and lemons, say the bells of St Clement's"? Again O'Brien nods. With a sort of grave courtesy he completes the stanza — St Clement's, St Martin's, Old Bailey, Shoreditch. "You knew the last line!" says Winston. Yes, he knew the last line. And now, O'Brien is afraid, it is time to go. But wait: Winston had better take one of these tablets. As Winston stands up O'Brien holds out a hand. His powerful grip crushes the bones of Winston's palm. At the door Winston looks back, but O'Brien seems already to be in process of putting him out of mind.
