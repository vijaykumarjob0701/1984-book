## Summary

...the gesture he resettles his spectacles, then goes on. What he had really intended to say was that in the article Winston used two words which have become obsolete — but only very recently. Has Winston seen the tenth edition of the Newspeak Dictionary? No: he did not think it had been issued yet; Records still uses the ninth. The tenth is not due for some months, O'Brien says, but a few advance copies have been circulated. He has one himself. It might interest Winston to look at it? Very much so, Winston says, immediately seeing where this is tending. Some of the new developments are most ingenious: the reduction in the number of verbs — that is the point that will appeal to Winston. Shall O'Brien send a messenger with the dictionary? He invariably forgets that kind of thing. Perhaps Winston could pick it up at O'Brien's flat at some time that suited him? Wait: let him give the address. They are standing in front of a telescreen. Somewhat absent-mindedly O'Brien feels two pockets, produces a small leather-covered notebook and a gold ink-pencil, and, immediately beneath the telescreen — in a position anyone watching could read — scribbles an address, tears out the page, and hands it over. He is usually at home in the evenings; if not, his servant will give Winston the dictionary. He is gone, leaving Winston holding the scrap. This time there is no need to conceal it. Nevertheless he carefully memorises what is written and, some hours later, drops it into the memory hole with a mass of other papers. They have talked for a couple of minutes at most. There is only one meaning the episode can have. It was contrived as a way of letting Winston know O'Brien's address.
