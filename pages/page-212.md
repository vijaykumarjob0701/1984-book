## Summary

The expression on his face changes. The annoyance passes and for a moment he looks almost pleased. A sort of intellectual warmth, the joy of the pedant who has found out some useless fact, shines through the dirt and scrubby hair. Has it ever occurred to Winston that the whole history of English poetry has been determined by the fact that the English language lacks rhymes? No, that particular thought has never occurred to Winston. Nor, in the circumstances, does it strike him as very important or interesting. Does Ampleforth know what time of day it is? Ampleforth looks startled again. He had hardly thought about it. They arrested him — it could be two days ago — perhaps three. His eyes flit round the walls as though he half expects a window. There is no difference between night and day in this place. He does not see how one can calculate the time. They talk desultorily for some minutes, then, without apparent reason, a yell from the telescreen bids them be silent. Winston sits quietly, hands crossed. Ampleforth, too large to sit in comfort on the narrow bench, fidgets from side to side, clasping his lank hands first round one knee, then the other. The telescreen barks at him to keep still. Time passes. Twenty minutes, an hour — it is difficult to judge. Once more there is a sound of boots outside. Winston's entrails contract. Soon, very soon, perhaps in five minutes, perhaps now, the tramp of boots will mean that his own turn has come. The door opens. The cold-faced young officer steps in. With a brief movement of the hand he indicates Ampleforth. "Room 101," he says. Ampleforth marches clumsily out between the guards, his face vaguely perturbed, but uncomprehending. What seems like a long time passes. The pain in Winston's belly has revived. His mind sags round and round on the same trick, like a ball falling again and again into the same series of slots. He has only six thoughts: the pain in his belly; a piece of bread; the blood and the screaming; O'Brien; Julia; the razor blade.
