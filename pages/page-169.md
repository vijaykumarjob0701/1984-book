## Summary

It is a heavy black volume, amateurishly bound, with no name or title on the cover. The print looks slightly irregular. The pages are worn at the edges and fall apart easily, as though the book has passed through many hands. The title-page names it *The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism*, by Emmanuel Goldstein. Winston begins reading Chapter I, "Ignorance is Strength." The opening argument, in the book's own terms, is that throughout recorded time — and probably since the end of the Neolithic Age — there have been three kinds of people: the High, the Middle, and the Low. They have been subdivided in many ways, borne countless different names, and changed in number and in attitude toward one another from age to age; but the essential structure of society has never altered. Even after enormous upheavals and seemingly irrevocable changes, the same pattern has always reasserted itself, as a gyroscope returns to equilibrium however far it is pushed. The aims of these three groups, the page breaks off, are entirely irreconcilable. Winston stops reading, chiefly in order to appreciate the fact that he is reading, in comfort and safety. He is alone: no telescreen, no ear at the keyhole, no nervous impulse to glance over his shoulder or cover the page with his hand. The sweet summer air plays against his cheek. From somewhere far away float the faint shouts of children; in the room itself there is no sound except the insect voice of the clock.
