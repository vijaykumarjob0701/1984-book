## Summary

...you could get the bugs out of it — but it would be a little cumbersome. He holds the lamp high. In the warm dim light the room looks curiously inviting. Winston thinks it would probably be easy to rent it for a few dollars a week, if he dared. The notion is wild and impossible, to be dropped at once, but the room wakes a nostalgia, an ancestral memory. He feels he knows exactly what it would be like to sit in that armchair by an open fire, feet in the fender, kettle on the hob: utterly alone, utterly secure, nobody watching, no voice pursuing, only the kettle singing and the clock ticking. He cannot help murmuring that there is no telescreen. The old man never had one — too expensive, and he never felt the need. He points out a gateleg table that would need new hinges if you used the flaps. Winston has already gravitated to a small bookcase. It holds only rubbish. The hunting-down of books has been as thorough in prole quarters as everywhere else. It is very unlikely any book printed before 1960 still exists in Oceania. The old man stands before a rosewood-framed picture opposite the bed and begins, delicately, that if Winston is interested in old prints... Winston examines it: a steel engraving of an oval building with rectangular windows, a small tower in front, a railing, and what seems a statue at the rear. It looks vaguely familiar, though he does not remember the statue.
