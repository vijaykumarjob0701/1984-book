## Summary

The owner agrees it is beautiful, but few would say so nowadays. If Winston wants it, the price is four dollars. He can remember when such a thing fetched eight pounds — a lot of money, though he cannot work out the comparison. Who cares about genuine antiques now, even the few that are left? Winston pays at once and slides the coveted object into his pocket. What draws him is less its beauty than the air of belonging to a different age. The soft rainwatery glass is unlike any he has seen. It is doubly attractive because it seems useless, though he guesses it was once a paperweight. It is heavy but makes little bulge. For a Party member it is a queer, even compromising thing: anything old, and anything beautiful, is vaguely suspect. The old man grows more cheerful; Winston realises he would have taken three or even two dollars. There is another room upstairs, the man says — not much in it. He lights another lamp and, bowed, leads the way up steep worn stairs and a tiny passage into a room that looks not on the street but on a cobbled yard and a forest of chimney-pots. The furniture is still arranged as if someone lived there: a strip of carpet, a picture or two, a deep slatternly armchair by the fireplace. An old-fashioned glass clock with a twelve-hour face ticks on the mantelpiece. Under the window an enormous bed still has its mattress.
