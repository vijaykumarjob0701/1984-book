...of an obscenity. Then, noticing she is sitting on something uneven, she slides off Winston's knees onto the bench. She begs his pardon — she would not have sat on him, only the guards put her there; they do not know how to treat a lady. She pats her breast, belches, says she is not quite herself, leans forward and vomits copiously on the floor. That is better, she says, leaning back with closed eyes. Never keep it down: get it up while it is fresh. She revives, turns to look at Winston, and seems immediately to take a fancy to him. She puts a vast arm round his shoulder and draws him towards her, breathing beer and vomit into his face. What is his name? Smith. Smith? That is funny. Her name is Smith too. Why, she adds sentimentally, she might be his mother! She might, thought Winston, be his mother. She is about the right age and physique, and it is probable that people change somewhat after twenty years in a forced-labour camp. No one else has spoken to him. To a surprising extent the ordinary criminals ignore the Party prisoners. "The polits," they call them, with a sort of uninterested contempt. The Party prisoners seem terrified of speaking to anybody, and above all of speaking to one another. Only once, when two Party members, both women, are pressed close together on the bench, he overhears amid the din a few hurriedly whispered words — and in particular a reference to something called "room one-oh-one," which he does not understand. It might be two or three hours ago that they brought him here. The dull pain in his belly never goes away, but sometimes it grows better and sometimes worse, and his thoughts expand or contract accordingly. When it grows worse he thinks only of the pain itself and of his desire for food. When it grows better, panic takes hold of him.
