## Summary

...is behind the wall. With a deadly effort, like wrenching a piece out of his own brain, he could even have dragged the thing into the open. He always wakes without discovering what it is; but somehow it is connected with what Julia had been saying when he cut her short. He's sorry — it's nothing. He doesn't like rats, that's all. Don't worry: they are not going to have the filthy brutes in here. She will stuff the hole with sacking before they go, and next time bring plaster and bung it up properly. Already the black instant of panic is half-forgotten. Slightly ashamed, he sits up against the bedhead. Julia gets out, pulls on overalls, and makes the coffee. The smell from the saucepan is so powerful and exciting they shut the window lest anyone outside notice and become inquisitive. Even better than the taste is the silky texture the sugar gives it, a thing Winston has almost forgotten after years of saccharine. With one hand in her pocket and bread and jam in the other, Julia wanders the room, glancing at the bookcase, pointing out how to repair the gateleg table, plumping herself into the ragged armchair to test it, examining the absurd twelve-hour clock with tolerant amusement. She brings the glass paperweight to the bed for better light. He takes it, fascinated as always by the soft rainwatery glass. What is it, does he think? He doesn't think it's anything — he doesn't think it was ever put to any use. That's what he likes. It's a little chunk of history they have forgotten to alter. It's a message from a hundred years ago, if one knew how to read it. And that picture — would that be a hundred years old? More. Two hundred, he dare say. One can't tell. It's impossible to discover the age of anything nowadays.
