## Summary

...were obvious, but the final motive is mysterious. He writes that he understands how, not why. He wonders again whether he himself is a lunatic. Perhaps a lunatic is simply a minority of one. Once it was madness to believe the earth goes round the sun; today it is madness to believe the past cannot be changed. If he is alone in that belief, he is a lunatic. Being mad does not greatly trouble him; the horror is that he might also be wrong. He looks at Big Brother's portrait in the front of the children's book. The hypnotic eyes gaze into his. A huge force seems to press into his skull, battering his brain, frightening him out of his beliefs, almost persuading him to deny his senses. In the end the Party will say two and two make five, and you will have to believe it. Their logic demands that claim. Their philosophy silently denies not only the value of experience but the existence of an outside world. The heresy of heresies is common sense. What terrifies him is not that they will kill you for thinking otherwise, but that they might be right. How do we know two and two make four, or that gravity works, or that the past cannot change? If past and world exist only in the mind, and the mind is controllable — what then? But no. His courage suddenly stiffens. O'Brien's face floats up, uninvited. He is more sure than before that O'Brien is on his side. He is writing the diary for O'Brien, to O'Brien — an endless letter nobody will read, but addressed to one person and coloured by that fact.
