## Summary

...the afternoon. The sun blazed; sweat tickled his face. And the thought struck him... "Why didn't you give her a good shove?" Julia says. "I would have." Yes, dear, she would have. He would have, if he had been the same person then as now. Or perhaps he would — he is not certain. Is he sorry he didn't? Yes. On the whole he is sorry he didn't. They sit side by side on the dusty floor. He pulls her closer; her head rests on his shoulder, the pleasant smell of her hair conquering the pigeon dung. She is very young, he thinks; she still expects something from life; she does not understand that pushing an inconvenient person over a cliff solves nothing. Actually it would have made no difference, he says. Then why is he sorry? Only because he prefers a positive to a negative. In this game they cannot win. Some kinds of failure are better than other kinds, that is all. Her shoulders wriggle with dissent. She always contradicts him when he talks like this. She will not accept it as a law of nature that the individual is always defeated. In a way she realises she is doomed, that sooner or later the Thought Police will catch and kill her, but with another part of her mind she believes you can construct a secret world and live as you choose. All you need is luck, cunning, and boldness. She does not understand that there is no such thing as happiness, that the only victory lies in the far future, long after you are dead, that from the moment of declaring war on the Party it is better to think of yourself as a corpse. "We are the dead," he says. "We're not dead yet," she answers prosaically. Not physically. Six months, a year — five years, conceivably. He is afraid of death. She is young, so presumably more afraid than he is. Obviously they will put it off as long as they can. But it makes very little difference. So long as human beings stay human, death and life are the same thing.
