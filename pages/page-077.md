## Summary

Chapter 8 opens in a side passage where the smell of real roasting coffee — not Victory Coffee — floats into the street. Winston stops without meaning to; for two seconds he is back in childhood. A slamming door cuts the smell off as if it were a sound. He has walked kilometres and his ulcer throbs. This is the second evening in three weeks he has skipped the Community Centre, a rash act, because attendances are checked. In principle a Party member has no spare time and is never alone except in bed. When not working, eating, or sleeping you are meant to join communal recreation; even a solitary walk is slightly dangerous. Newspeak calls it ownlife: individualism and eccentricity. But the mild April air tempted him. The warmer blue sky made the Centre's long noisy evening — exhausting games, lectures, gin-oiled fake friendship — feel intolerable. On impulse he left the bus-stop and wandered the maze of London, south, east, then north, barely caring where he went. The diary line about hope lying in the proles keeps returning: it feels both like a mystical truth and an obvious absurdity. He is in brown slums north-east of what was once Saint Pancras Station, on a cobbled street of little two-storey houses whose battered doorways open on the pavement like ratholes. Filthy puddles lie among the stones. People pour in and out of dark doorways and down narrow alleys.
