## Summary

...faint surprise, because he has done so only two or three hours ago. He notices that the clock on the mantelpiece says nine, meaning twenty-one. But the light seems too strong. Would not the light be fading at twenty-one hours on an August evening? He wonders whether after all he and Julia have mistaken the time — have slept the clock round and thought it was twenty-thirty when really it was nought eight-thirty on the following morning. But he does not pursue the thought further. It is not interesting. There is another, lighter step in the passage. Mr Charrington comes into the room. The demeanour of the black-uniformed men suddenly becomes more subdued. Something has also changed in Mr Charrington's appearance. His eye falls on the fragments of the glass paperweight. "Pick up those pieces," he says sharply. A man stoops to obey. The cockney accent has disappeared; Winston suddenly realizes whose voice it was that he heard a few moments ago on the telescreen. Mr Charrington is still wearing his old velvet jacket, but his hair, which had been almost white, has turned black. Also he is not wearing his spectacles. He gives Winston a single sharp glance, as though verifying his identity, and then pays no more attention to him. He is still recognizable, but he is not the same person any longer. His body has straightened and seems to have grown bigger. His face has undergone only tiny changes that have nevertheless worked a complete transformation. The black eyebrows are less bushy, the wrinkles are gone, the whole lines of the face seem to have altered; even the nose seems shorter. It is the alert, cold face of a man of about five-and-thirty. It occurs to Winston that for the first time in his life he is looking, with knowledge, at a member of the Thought Police. Part Two ends on that recognition.
