## Summary

...pleasures as that you should want to avoid being caught. She hates the Party and says so in the crudest words, but she makes no general criticism. Except where it touches her own life she has no interest in doctrine. She never uses Newspeak except words that have passed into everyday speech. She has never heard of the Brotherhood and refuses to believe in it. Any organised revolt, bound to fail, strikes her as stupid. The clever thing is to break the rules and stay alive all the same. Winston wonders how many others like her there are in the younger generation: people who grew up in the Revolution's world, accept the Party as unalterable like the sky, and do not rebel against its authority so much as evade it, as a rabbit dodges a dog. They do not discuss marriage. It is too remote. No imaginable committee would sanction it even if Katharine could somehow be got rid of. It is hopeless even as a daydream. What was she like, his wife? Does Julia know the Newspeak word goodthinkful — naturally orthodox, incapable of a bad thought? She did not know the word, but she knows the kind of person. He starts the story of his marriage, but curiously she seems to know the essential parts already. She describes, almost as if she had seen it, Katharine stiffening at a touch and still seeming to push him away with all her strength even while her arms were clasped round him. With Julia he has no difficulty talking about such things. Katharine has long ceased to be a painful memory and become merely a distasteful one.
