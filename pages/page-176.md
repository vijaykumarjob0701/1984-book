## Summary

...that there is a chronic shortage of half the necessities of life; but this is looked on as an advantage. It is deliberate policy to keep even the favoured groups somewhere near the brink of hardship, because a general state of scarcity increases the importance of small privileges and thus magnifies the distinction between one group and another. By early-twentieth-century standards even an Inner Party member lives an austere, laborious life. Nevertheless the few luxuries he does enjoy — a large well-appointed flat, better clothes, better food, drink and tobacco, two or three servants, a private motor-car or helicopter — set him in a different world from an Outer Party member, and the Outer Party has a similar advantage over the submerged masses called the proles. The social atmosphere is that of a besieged city, where a lump of horseflesh makes the difference between wealth and poverty. And the consciousness of being at war, and therefore in danger, makes handing all power to a small caste seem the natural, unavoidable condition of survival. War accomplishes the necessary destruction, but in a psychologically acceptable way. In principle it would be simple to waste the world's surplus labour by building temples and pyramids, digging holes and filling them up, or producing vast quantities of goods and then setting fire to them. But that would provide only the economic, not the emotional, basis for a hierarchical society. What matters here is not the morale of the masses, whose attitude is unimportant so long as they are kept at work, but the morale of the Party itself. Even the humblest Party member is expected to be competent, industrious, and even intelligent within narrow limits, but he must also be a credulous and ignorant fanatic whose prevailing moods are fear, hatred, adulation, and orgiastic triumph.
