## Summary

That is as far as the old man can get. A farthing, he explains, was a small copper coin, something like a cent. Winston asks where St Martin's was. It is still standing, in Victory Square beside the picture gallery: a building with a triangular porch, pillars, and a big flight of steps. Winston knows it well — now a museum for propaganda: scale models of rocket bombs and Floating Fortresses, waxwork scenes of enemy atrocities, and the like. It used to be called St Martin-in-the-Fields, the man adds, though he remembers no fields. Winston does not buy the picture. It would be an even more incongruous possession than the paperweight, and impossible to carry home unless taken from its frame. He lingers, talking. The owner's name is not Weeks, as the shop-front suggests, but Charrington: a widower of sixty-three who has kept the shop thirty years and always meant to change the sign, but never quite did. All the while the half-remembered rhyme runs through Winston's head. When you say it you have the illusion of hearing bells — the bells of a lost London that still exists somewhere, disguised and forgotten. From one ghostly steeple after another they seem to peal. Yet so far as he can remember he has never in real life heard church bells. He goes downstairs alone so Charrington will not see him reconnoitring the street before he steps out. He has already decided that after a suitable interval — a month, say — he will risk the shop again. It is perhaps no more dangerous than skipping an evening at the Centre. The serious piece of folly...
