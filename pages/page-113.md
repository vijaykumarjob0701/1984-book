## Summary

...better to go quietly. They reach the edge of the little wood. She stops him: do not go into the open; someone might be watching. They are all right behind the boughs. They stand in the shade of hazel bushes. Sunlight filtering through countless leaves is still hot on their faces. Winston looks into the field beyond and undergoes a slow shock of recognition. He knows it by sight: an old close-bitten pasture, a wandering footpath, molehills, a ragged hedge, elm boughs swaying, leaves stirring in dense masses like women's hair. Surely nearby, out of sight, there must be a stream with green pools and dace? He whispers the question. Yes — at the edge of the next field, big fish under the willows, waving their tails. "It's the Golden Country — almost," he murmurs. She asks what that is. Nothing, really: a landscape he has sometimes seen in a dream. "Look!" A thrush has alighted on a bough not five metres away, almost at face level. Perhaps it has not seen them. It is in the sun, they in the shade. It spreads its wings, fits them carefully back, ducks its head as if making an obeisance to the sun, then pours forth a torrent of song. In the afternoon hush the volume is startling. They cling together, fascinated. The music goes on and on, minute after minute, with astonishing variations, never repeating, as if the bird were showing off its virtuosity. Sometimes it stops, resettles its wings, swells its speckled breast, and bursts into song again. Winston watches with a sort of vague reverence. For whom, for what, is that bird singing? No mate, no rival is watching. What makes it sit at the edge of the lonely wood and pour its music into nothingness?
