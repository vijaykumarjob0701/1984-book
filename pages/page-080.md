## Summary

They are not arguing about the war. One man swears no lottery number ending in seven has won for over fourteen months; he has two years of results written down. The other insists a seven did win — 407, second week of February. The third tells them to pack it in. Winston looks back: they still argue with passionate faces. The Lottery, with its huge weekly prizes, is the one public event proles take seriously. For millions it may be the main reason to stay alive — their delight, their folly, their painkiller, their mental stimulant. Even people who can barely read can do intricate calculations and astonishing memory tricks about it. A whole tribe lives by selling systems, forecasts, and lucky charms. The Ministry of Plenty runs the Lottery. Winston, like every Party member, knows the big prizes are largely imaginary: only small sums are paid, and the big winners are non-existent persons. With no real communication between parts of Oceania, that is easy to arrange. Still he tells himself that if there is hope it lies in the proles; you have to cling to that. In words it sounds reasonable; looking at the actual people on the pavement, it becomes an act of faith. His new street runs downhill. He feels he has been near here before and that a main road is not far. Ahead comes a din of shouting.
