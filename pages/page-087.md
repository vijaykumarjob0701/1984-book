## Summary

...but not large ones. When memory fails and written records are falsified, the Party's claim to have improved life has to be accepted, because no standard against which to test it exists, or can ever exist again. His train of thought stops. He is in a narrow street of dark little shops mixed with houses. Above his head hang three discoloured metal balls that once looked gilded. He knows the place: the junk-shop where he bought the diary. Fear twinges. Buying the book was rash enough; he had sworn never to come near again. Yet the instant his thoughts wandered, his feet brought him back. It was just such suicidal impulses he hoped the diary would guard him against. It is nearly twenty-one hours, but the shop is still open. Feeling less conspicuous inside than hanging about, he steps in. If questioned he can plausibly say he wanted razor blades. The owner has just lit a hanging oil lamp with an unclean but friendly smell. He is about sixty, frail and bowed, with a long kind nose and mild eyes distorted by thick glasses. His hair is almost white, his eyebrows still black and bushy. The glasses, fussy gentle movements, and aged black velvet jacket give him a vague air of a literary man or musician. His voice is soft, faded; his accent is less debased than most proles'. He says at once that he recognised Winston: the gentleman who bought the young lady's keepsake album — beautiful cream-laid paper, not made for perhaps fifty years. He peers over his spectacles.
