## Summary

Yet a faint air of disreputability clings to Syme. He says things better left unsaid, has read too many books, and goes to the Chestnut Tree Café, a haunt of painters and musicians. There is no law against it, even unwritten, but the place feels ill-omened: old discredited Party leaders gathered there before they were purged, and Goldstein was rumoured to have been seen there long ago. Syme's fate is easy to foresee. Still, if he understood Winston's secret opinions for three seconds he would betray him at once — as would anyone, but Syme more than most. Zeal is not enough. Orthodoxy is unconsciousness. Syme looks up: here comes Parsons, the tone adding "that bloody fool." Parsons, Winston's Victory Mansions neighbour, threads through the room — tubby, fair, frog-faced. At thirty-five he is growing fat at neck and waist, but he moves like a boy. He looks like a large child; you almost see him in Spies uniform, dimpled knees and pudgy arms. He always reverts to shorts for hikes. He sits with a cheery greeting and a powerful sweat-smell; his pink face is beaded. At the Community Centre you could tell he had played table-tennis by the damp bat handle. Syme studies a long column of words with an ink-pencil.
