Between the super-states' frontiers, and not permanently owned by any of them, lies a rough quadrilateral with corners at Tangier, Brazzaville, Darwin, and Hong Kong, holding about a fifth of the earth's population. It is for these thickly populated regions, and for the northern ice-cap, that the three powers constantly struggle. In practice no one power ever controls the whole disputed area. Portions keep changing hands, and the chance of seizing a fragment by a sudden stroke of treachery dictates the endless changes of alignment. The disputed territories contain valuable minerals and some important vegetable products, such as rubber, which colder climates must synthesize at high cost. But above all they contain a bottomless reserve of cheap labour. Whichever power controls equatorial Africa, the Middle East, Southern India, or the Indonesian Archipelago also controls the bodies of scores or hundreds of millions of ill-paid, hard-driven workers. The inhabitants of these areas, reduced more or less openly to the status of slaves, pass continually from conqueror to conqueror and are expended like coal or oil in a race to turn out more armaments, capture more territory, control more labour, and so on indefinitely. The fighting never really moves beyond the edges of the disputed areas. Eurasia's frontiers flow between the Congo basin and the northern Mediterranean; Indian Ocean and Pacific islands are constantly captured and recaptured; the Mongolia dividing line is never stable; around the Pole all three claim huge, largely empty territories.
