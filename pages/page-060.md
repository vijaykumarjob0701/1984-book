## Summary

Chapter 6 opens with Winston writing in his diary about an evening three years earlier, on a dark side-street near a big railway station. A woman stood in a doorway under a weak lamp. Her young face was thickly painted; it was the paint that drew him — white like a mask, bright red lips. Party women never paint their faces. The street was empty, with no telescreens. She asked for two dollars. He cannot go on. He shuts his eyes and tries to squeeze the recurring picture away. He wants to shout filthy words, bang his head, kick the table, throw the inkpot — any violent act that might black out the memory. He reflects that your worst enemy is your own nervous system: inner tension can become a visible symptom at any moment. Weeks ago he passed an ordinary-looking Party man whose left cheek suddenly spasmed, then twitched again as they passed — habitual, perhaps unconscious. Winston thought the man was finished. The deadliest danger is talking in your sleep; he sees no way to guard against that. He draws breath and goes on writing.
