## Summary

She goes over to look at the picture. Here's where that brute stuck his nose out, she says, kicking the wainscoting under it. What is this place? She's seen it before somewhere. It's a church, or used to be — St Clement Danes. The fragment of rhyme Charrington taught him comes back, and he adds half-nostalgically: oranges and lemons, say the bells of St Clement's. To his astonishment she caps the line: you owe me three farthings, say the bells of St Martin's; when will you pay me? say the bells of Old Bailey. She can't remember how it goes on, but it ends with a candle to light you to bed and a chopper to chop off your head. It is like the two halves of a countersign. There must be another line after Old Bailey. Perhaps it could be dug out of Charrington's memory if he were suitably prompted. Who taught her that? Her grandfather. He used to say it when she was a little girl. He was vaporized when she was eight — at any rate, he disappeared. She wonders what a lemon was. She's seen oranges: a kind of round yellow fruit with a thick skin. Winston can remember lemons. They were quite common in the fifties, so sour they set your teeth on edge even to smell them. She bets the picture has bugs behind it; she'll take it down and clean it some day. She supposes it's almost time to leave. She must start washing this paint off. What a bore! She'll get the lipstick off his face afterwards. Winston does not get up for a few minutes more. The room is darkening. He turns toward the light and lies gazing into the paperweight. The inexhaustibly interesting thing is not the fragment of coral but the interior of the glass itself. There is such a depth of it, and yet it is almost as transparent as air.
