## Summary

The frame is fixed to the wall, the old man says, but he could unscrew it. Winston finally knows the building: it is a ruin now, in the street outside the Palace of Justice. That is right — outside the Law Courts. It was bombed many years ago. It was once a church, St Clement Danes. The man smiles as if saying something slightly ridiculous and adds the rhyme: oranges and lemons, say the bells of St Clement's. Winston asks what that is. A rhyme from the man's boyhood, ending with a candle to light you to bed and a chopper to chop off your head. It was a kind of dance: children held arms up for you to pass under and dropped them on the last line. It was just names of the principal London churches. Winston wonders what century the church belongs to. A London building's age is always hard to fix. Anything large and impressive, if it looks reasonably new, is automatically claimed as post-Revolution; anything obviously older is pushed into a dim "Middle Ages." The centuries of capitalism are held to have produced nothing of value. You cannot learn history from architecture any more than from books. Statues, inscriptions, memorial stones, street names — anything that might throw light on the past has been systematically altered. Winston says he never knew it had been a church. The old man says many are left, put to other uses, and recovers more of the rhyme: oranges and lemons... you owe me three farthings, say the bells of St Martin's.
