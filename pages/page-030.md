## Summary

He sees that tragedy belonged to an older time of privacy, love, friendship, and families who stood together without needing a reason. His mother's memory hurts because she died loving him when he was too young and selfish to love her back, and because she gave herself to a private, unchangeable loyalty. Today there is fear, hatred, and pain, but no dignity or deep, complex sorrow. He reads that in their eyes as they sink hundreds of fathoms. The dream jumps: he stands on springy turf in gilded evening light, in a landscape so familiar he cannot tell if it is real. Awake he calls it the Golden Country — a rabbit-bitten pasture, a path, molehills, a ragged hedge, elms moving like women's hair, an unseen stream with dace under willows. The dark-haired girl comes across the field, tears off her clothes in one careless movement, and throws them aside. Her body does not stir desire. What overwhelms him is the gesture, which seems to wipe out a whole culture, as if Party and Thought Police could be swept away by one arm movement. That too feels ancient. He wakes with the word "Shakespeare" on his lips. An ear-splitting telescreen whistle starts; it is nought.
