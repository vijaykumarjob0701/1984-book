## Summary

The Party histories might still be true, after a fashion — even completely true. He tries once more. The man has lived half his life before the Revolution; in 1925 he was already grown. From what he remembers, was life then better or worse? If he could choose, then or now? The old man looks at the dartboard, finishes his beer more slowly, and speaks with a tolerant, mellowed air. He knows what Winston expects: that he would sooner be young again. Most people would. When you are young you have health and strength; at his age he is never well — terrible feet, bladder six or seven times a night. On the other hand, old age has advantages: fewer worries, and no truck with women for nearly thirty years, nor any want of them. Winston sits back. It is no use. He is about to buy more beer when the old man shuffles into the stinking urinal; the extra half-litre is already working. Winston stares at his empty glass and hardly notices his feet carrying him back into the street. Within twenty years at most, he thinks, the huge simple question — was life better before the Revolution? — will have ceased to be answerable. In effect it is unanswerable now: the few survivors cannot compare one age with another. They remember a million useless things — a quarrel, a lost bicycle pump, a dead sister's face, dust on a windy morning seventy years ago — but all the relevant facts lie outside their vision. They are like the ant, which can see small objects...
