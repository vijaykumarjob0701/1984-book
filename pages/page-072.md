## Summary

...of the Thought Police ever escaped in the end. They were corpses waiting to be sent back to the grave. Nobody sat at nearby tables; it was unwise even to be seen near them. They sat silent over clove-flavoured gin, the café's speciality. Rutherford impressed Winston most. He had been a famous caricaturist whose brutal cartoons helped inflame opinion before and during the Revolution. His rare *Times* cartoons now only copy that old manner and feel lifeless: slums, starving children, street battles, capitalists in top hats — even on the barricades they still cling to the hats, a hopeless effort to climb back into the past. Rutherford is monstrous, with a mane of greasy grey hair, a pouched, seamed face. He must once have been hugely strong; now the great body sags and bulges, breaking up like a crumbling mountain. It was the lonely hour of fifteen. Winston cannot remember why he was there. The café was almost empty; tinny music trickled from the telescreens. The three men barely moved. Unasked, the waiter brought more gin. A chessboard sat beside them with pieces set out but no game started. Then, for perhaps half a minute, the telescreens changed. The tune and tone shifted into a cracked, braying, jeering sound Winston thinks of as a yellow note. A voice sang that under the spreading chestnut tree he sold you and you sold me.
