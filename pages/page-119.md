## Summary

...have been hard to find time. Winston's week is sixty hours, Julia's even longer, and their free days rarely coincide. She seldom has a completely free evening. She spends an astonishing amount of time at lectures and demonstrations, distributing Junior Anti-Sex League literature, preparing Hate Week banners, collecting for the savings campaign, and the like. It pays, she says: it is camouflage. If you keep the small rules, you can break the big ones. She even induces Winston to mortgage another evening by enrolling for voluntary part-time munition work. So one evening a week he spends four hours of paralysing boredom screwing together bits of metal that are probably bomb-fuse parts, in a draughty, ill-lit workshop where hammers mix drearily with telescreen music. When they meet in the church tower the gaps in their fragmentary talk are filled. It is a blazing afternoon. The little square chamber above the bells is hot and stagnant and smells overpoweringly of pigeon dung. They sit for hours on the dusty, twig-littered floor, one or other getting up now and then to glance through the arrowslits and make sure no one is coming. Julia is twenty-six. She lives in a hostel with thirty other girls ("Always in the stink of women! How I hate women!") and works, as he guessed, on the novel-writing machines in Fiction. She enjoys the work, which is mainly running and servicing a powerful, tricky electric motor. She is "not clever" but fond of using her hands and at home with machinery. She can describe the whole process of composing a novel, from the Planning Committee's directive to the Rewrite Squad's final touching-up. She is not interested in the finished product. She doesn't much care for reading. Books are just a commodity that has to be produced, like jam or bootlaces.
