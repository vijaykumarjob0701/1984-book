## Summary

The book continues mid-sentence from the last page. Acts such as reducing whole populations to slavery, and reprisals that go as far as boiling or burying prisoners alive, are treated as normal — and, when one's own side commits them, even meritorious. In a physical sense, though, the war involves very small numbers of people, mostly highly trained specialists, and causes comparatively few casualties. Fighting, when it happens at all, takes place on vague frontiers the average person can only guess at, or around the Floating Fortresses that guard sea lanes. In the centres of civilization war means little more than a continuous shortage of consumer goods and the occasional rocket bomb that may kill a few scores of people. War has changed its character: more exactly, the reasons for waging it have changed their order of importance. Motives that were already present in the great wars of the early twentieth century are now dominant and consciously acted on. To understand the present war — for despite regroupings every few years it is always the same war — one must first realize that it cannot be decisive. None of the three super-states could be definitively conquered even by the other two together. They are too evenly matched, and their natural defences are too strong: Eurasia by vast land spaces, Oceania by the width of the Atlantic and Pacific, Eastasia by the fecundity and industriousness of its inhabitants. Secondly, there is no longer, in a material sense, anything to fight about. Self-contained economies have ended the old scramble for markets, and competition for raw materials is no longer life-or-death. Each super-state is vast enough to get almost all it needs inside its own boundaries. In so far as the war has a direct economic purpose, it is a war for labour...
