## Summary

The owner asks if Winston wants anything special, or is just looking. Winston says he was passing and wants nothing in particular. Just as well, the man says apologetically: he could not have satisfied him. Between them, the antique trade is about finished — no demand, no stock. Furniture, china, and glass have been broken up by degrees; metal has mostly been melted down. He has not seen a brass candlestick in years. The tiny shop is uncomfortably full, but almost nothing has any value. Dusty picture-frames are stacked around the walls. In the window are trays of nuts and bolts, worn chisels, broken penknives, tarnished watches that do not even pretend to work, and other rubbish. Only a small corner table holds odds and ends — lacquered snuffboxes, agate brooches — that might include something interesting. Winston's eye is caught by a round, smooth thing gleaming in the lamplight. It is a heavy lump of glass, curved on one side, flat on the other, almost a hemisphere. Colour and texture have a rainwater softness. At the heart, magnified by the curve, is a strange pink, folded object like a rose or a sea anemone. Fascinated, Winston asks what it is. Coral, the old man says, probably from the Indian Ocean, embedded in glass at least a hundred years ago, maybe more. Winston says it is a beautiful thing.
